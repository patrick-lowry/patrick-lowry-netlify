## Small Victories

Week two, and I've discovered my one competitive advantage: I speak English natively.

Not that it helps much when the "English" being spoken is things like:

*"The Jacobian of the transformation yields a computationally tractable solution to the optimization problem."*

I mean, those are English words. Technically. But so is "antidisestablishmentarianism" and I don't know what that means either.

## The International Cohort

Here's what I've learned about my classmates:

**Li**: Speaks Mandarin, English, and Python. Has a mathematics degree from Beijing. Can derive equations faster than I can Google them.

**Andreas**: German, obviously brilliant, speaks three languages fluently. Corrected the professor once (he was right).

**Priya**: Did her undergrad in Mumbai, Masters in Paris. Speaks Hindi, English, French, and apparently mathematics is her fourth language.

**Me**: Speaks English. Once ordered food in Spanish. That's it. That's my language portfolio.

## The Language Barrier (That Isn't)

The funny part? The international students are struggling less than me.

**Andreas**: "Sorry, my English is not so good."
*Proceeds to give a perfect 10-minute presentation on gradient descent with zero mistakes*

**Me**: Native speaker.
*Can barely explain what a for-loop does without stuttering*

## Technical English

I've realized there are actually two English languages:

1. **Regular English**: What I speak
2. **Academic Machine Learning English**: A completely different language that just happens to use the same alphabet

Examples of Academic ML English:
- "Trivial" (translation: incredibly complex but I understand it)
- "Obviously" (translation: this took me three hours to figure out)
- "Straightforward" (translation: I have no idea why this works but it does)
- "As we all know" (translation: I forgot to explain this, hope you figured it out)

## The Great Equalizer

The one time we're all on the same level? When the professor starts writing code.

Doesn't matter if you speak five languages. Doesn't matter if you have a PhD in mathematics. When you get a `KeyError` in Python, we all suffer together.

That's when I feel most at home. Not because I'm good at debugging, but because finally, FINALLY, everyone looks as confused as I feel.

## The Real Lesson

Being a native English speaker in a British university: Useful

Understanding English when it's being used to explain measure theory: Not useful

Being able to say "I have no idea what's happening" in perfect English: Priceless

*Still here, still confused, but at least I can articulate my confusion clearly...*
